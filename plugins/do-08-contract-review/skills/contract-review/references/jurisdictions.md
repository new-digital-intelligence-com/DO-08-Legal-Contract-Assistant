# Where governing law changes the answer

A clause is not enforceable because it is written down. These are the places where the governing
law overrides the drafting, so a review that reports the clause without the jurisdiction is
reporting half the fact.

**This is orientation, not advice.** Enforceability turns on facts — where the employee works,
where the contract is performed, which entity signs — that a review does not have. Flag the issue
and name it as a question for counsel; never conclude that a clause is void or safe.

## Non-competes, United States

| Jurisdiction | Position |
|---|---|
| California | Void for employees, with very narrow exceptions around the sale of a business. Also unlawful to *attempt* to enforce, and to require an employee to sign one |
| North Dakota | Void, similar sale-of-business exception |
| Oklahoma | Void, except a narrow customer non-solicit |
| Minnesota | Void for agreements entered from 1 July 2023 |
| Colorado, Illinois, Washington, Oregon, Maine, Maryland, Virginia, Nevada, Rhode Island, Massachusetts | Enforceable only above a salary threshold, with notice requirements, and often with garden-leave or consideration rules. Thresholds change yearly |
| Most other states | Reasonableness test — duration, geography, scope, and a legitimate protectable interest |

Two things routinely missed. First, the sale-of-business context is treated far more permissively
almost everywhere than the employment context, so the same words in an M&A agreement and an
employment agreement are not the same clause. Second, a choice-of-law clause picking a permissive
state does not reliably save a non-compete against an employee working in a prohibitive one —
California in particular applies its own rule.

## Non-competes and restraints outside the US

- **United Kingdom** — enforceable only so far as reasonably necessary to protect a legitimate
  interest. Six to twelve months is the usual outer edge for senior staff. Garden leave is often
  set off against the restraint period.
- **Germany** — a post-termination non-compete requires compensation of at least 50% of last
  remuneration for its duration. Without it the clause is unenforceable, and the employer can be
  bound to pay if the employee elects to observe it.
- **France** — requires a financial counterpart, limits in time and space, and a legitimate
  interest.
- **India, Singapore** — post-employment restraints are narrowly construed; India in particular
  treats most as void restraints of trade.

## Choice of law

- **Delaware** — corporate-friendly, deep and predictable case law, the default for US commercial
  and M&A work.
- **New York** — the norm for finance and sophisticated commercial agreements; its courts will
  respect a choice of NY law even without a NY nexus above a value threshold.
- **California** — employee- and consumer-protective, and it applies its own mandatory rules to
  California workers whatever the contract says.
- **England and Wales** — a common neutral choice for cross-border commercial deals.
- **BVI, Cayman, Seychelles, Marshall Islands** — treat as a red flag on an operating contract.
  The concern is not the substantive law but the cost and delay of enforcing anything, and the
  difficulty of finding assets. Common and unremarkable in fund and holding-company structures;
  unusual in a supply agreement.

## Arbitration

- **AAA, JAMS** — standard US commercial. Predictable, and expensive at scale.
- **ICC, LCIA, SIAC, HKIAC** — international; SIAC and HKIAC for Asia-Pacific counterparties.
  Costs run well into six figures for a substantial dispute, which effectively removes small
  claims as a remedy.
- **Seat matters more than the rules.** The seat determines the supervising court and the
  annulment route. A "London seat, ICC rules" clause and a "Paris seat, ICC rules" clause behave
  differently.
- **Mandatory arbitration plus a class-action waiver** materially limits remedies. In consumer and
  employment contexts it also runs into local restrictions that vary widely.
- **Check the carve-out for injunctive relief.** Without one, a party cannot go to a court quickly
  to stop a confidentiality breach, which is the one situation where speed is the remedy.

## Data protection

- **EU and UK GDPR** — an international transfer needs a named mechanism: adequacy, Standard
  Contractual Clauses, or the UK addendum. "The parties will comply with applicable law" is not a
  mechanism. Check the SCCs are actually attached and the right module is selected.
- **US state privacy laws** — California, Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah and a growing list
  impose contract terms on service providers. A DPA drafted only for GDPR often misses them.
- **Sectoral regimes** — HIPAA needs a Business Associate Agreement; GLBA, FERPA and PCI-DSS each
  carry their own required terms. A general DPA does not satisfy any of them.

## Consumer and small-business protective rules

Where the counterparty is a consumer or, in some jurisdictions, a small business, mandatory law
can strike out terms the contract states plainly:

- **UK** — the Unfair Contract Terms Act and the Consumer Rights Act limit exclusions of liability;
  liability for death or personal injury from negligence cannot be excluded at all.
- **EU** — the Unfair Terms Directive, plus national implementations.
- **Australia** — the unfair contract terms regime now reaches standard-form small-business
  contracts, with penalties.

If the counterparty might be a consumer or a small business on standard terms, say so — an
exclusion clause that reads as watertight may not survive.
